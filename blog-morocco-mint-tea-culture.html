Morocco's Mint Tea Culture, Explained

In Morocco, mint tea is less a beverage than a ritual — a standard gesture of hospitality offered to guests, business visitors, and travelers alike, almost everywhere you go.

Culture · 5 min read · Morocco Boutique Tours

More Than a Drink

Known locally as atay, Moroccan mint tea is often called "Berber whisky" — a nod to how central it is to daily social life, despite containing no alcohol at all. It's served to welcome guests into a home, to open a business conversation, to close a carpet negotiation in the souks, and simply throughout the day among family and friends. Turning down a glass, while not offensive, is unusual enough that most visitors find it easier — and more rewarding — to simply accept.

How It's Made

The base is Chinese gunpowder green tea, brewed strong, combined with a generous handful of fresh spearmint and a notable amount of sugar — traditional recipes are far sweeter than most Western palates expect, though you can always ask for less sugar. The tea is brewed and poured in small batches, often refreshed at the table rather than served all at once from a single large pot.

The Pouring Ritual

The most recognizable part of the ritual is the pour itself: tea is poured from a considerable height above the glass, sometimes a foot or more, to aerate the liquid and create a light foam on top — a visual marker of a well-made glass. It's a small skill in itself, and watching an experienced host pour without spilling a drop is part of the experience.

Tea Among the Nomads

On desert routes through the Sahara, you'll often be welcomed into a nomadic family's tent for a glass of tea as part of a guided excursion — a genuine, unhurried gesture of hospitality rather than a staged photo opportunity, and one of the most memorable small moments many travelers take from a Morocco trip.

Etiquette When You're Offered a Glass

Accepting is the polite default, even if you only take a few sips. It's common to be offered a second or even third glass over the course of a visit — Moroccan hospitality tends to be generous rather than brief — and a warm thank-you goes a long way regardless of how much you actually drink.

Frequently Asked

Moroccan Mint Tea: Quick Answers

Why is Moroccan tea poured from high up?

Pouring from height aerates the tea and creates a light foam on the surface, considered a visual sign the tea has been made and poured well. It also helps cool the tea slightly before serving.

Is Moroccan mint tea very sweet?

Traditionally, yes — recipes typically use more sugar than most Western visitors expect. You can politely ask for less sugar, and most hosts are happy to accommodate.

Should I accept tea when it's offered in Morocco?

Generally yes. Accepting a glass of tea is the polite response to what is a genuine gesture of hospitality, whether offered in a home, a shop, or a nomad's tent in the desert.
